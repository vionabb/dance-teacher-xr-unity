## 9 Discussion

<a id="chap:discussion"></a>

Taken together, the four main case studies describe a progression in which the project moves from symbolic feedback toward increasingly general and adaptive forms of movement teaching. [Reference](04-teaching-american-sign-language-in-mixed-reality.md#chap:ubicomp2019) showed that automated movement feedback becomes much more useful when the system operates through an interpretable representation rather than raw sensing alone, but it also showed the limits of a highly domain-specific notation system. [Reference](05-decomposition-and-structured-representation-of-human-motion-capture.md#chap:iser2020) then pursued a more movement-general representation strategy by decomposing raw motion capture data into temporally and skeletally structured units. [Reference](06-enhancing-the-educational-potential-of-tiktok-dance-videos.md#chap:chi-tiktok-dance) operationalized that broader agenda at scale, demonstrating that online movement media can be transformed into structured learning experiences whose pedagogical design matters at least as much as their visual assets. Finally, [Reference](07-toward-an-adaptive-virtual-dance-coach.md#chap:chi27-dancecoach) tested what happened when that trajectory was extended toward adaptive coaching and revealed a change in what most constrained the system. This progression is consistent with prior work on adaptive lesson generation and augmented feedback, where systems first impose structured guidance and only then support more responsive or individualized instruction ([Yang et al. 2013](11-references.md#ref-yang2013twophasedancelesson); [Sigrist et al. 2013a](11-references.md#ref-sigrist2013augmented)).

Across these cases, the central difficulty shifts. Interpretable representations and structured pedagogy make systems legible and scalable, but they are not sufficient for coaching. Once learners understand what to do and how to practice, the bottleneck becomes evaluation: whether the system can produce feedback that is specific, trustworthy, and instructionally useful. This reframes the problem of movement-teaching systems from one of representation and presentation to one of meaningful assessment and adaptation.

### 9.1 Shared Findings Across Case Studies

#### 9.1.1 Interpretable representations enable legible feedback.

The early ASL system established a pattern that recurs throughout the thesis: learner-facing feedback becomes more useful when the system operates through an intermediate representation that makes motion analyzable in human-meaningful terms, a principle reflected in prior movement-analysis and qualitative representation work ([Guest 2013](11-references.md#ref-guest2013labanotation); [Camurri, Volpe, et al. 2016](11-references.md#ref-camurri2016dancer)). HNS provided one such representation, and the IMR work pursued a more general version of that idea. In these systems, this emerged less as a theoretical commitment than as a practical constraint.

This does not imply that explicit representations are strictly necessary. It remains possible that sufficiently large-scale machine learning systems could learn to produce legible feedback directly from raw motion data without explicitly defined intermediate representations. Such approaches would likely depend on large, high-quality datasets pairing motion with human-interpretable feedback, which are not currently widely available in expressive movement domains. The systems in this thesis therefore operate in a regime where explicit representations remain necessary to bridge sensing and instruction.

#### 9.1.2 Pedagogical structure matters more than additional visual aids in video-based learning systems.

The TikTok dance chapter showed that simply exposing a learner to movement media, even with additional visual aids layered on top, is not enough. What mattered most was the organization of practice: segmentation, repetition, fading guidance, and the arrangement of activities into a learning journey. Within this class of video-based systems, pedagogical structure appeared to contribute more to learning outcomes than incremental refinements to visual presentation alone. Prior systems similarly emphasize structured progression, staged practice, and task decomposition as central design elements rather than treating presentation alone as sufficient ([Wang et al. 2025](11-references.md#ref-wang2025_mrdrum); [Weng et al. 2025](11-references.md#ref-weng2025_sop_basketball)).

Participatory design work on accessible dance instruction independently surfaces a set of structural requirements that closely mirror this trajectory. That work identifies the need to decompose routines into named movement units, organize instruction into staged learning phases, and distribute information across modalities such as narration, sound, and haptics, while also separating the problem of conveying what to do from the problem of conveying how to do it ([Das et al. 2026](11-references.md#ref-das2026accessibleasynchronousdance)).

This convergence suggests that these structures are not artifacts of a particular implementation, but reflect more general constraints on how complex movement can be made learnable. The systems in this thesis can therefore be understood as one computational instantiation of a design space that also emerges through human-centered design processes.

#### 9.1.3 Automation enables pedagogy rather than replacing it.

Automation mattered only insofar as it enabled pedagogy.

Learners already have access to vast amounts of online video and can construct their own practice routines. The motivation for automation in this work was instead to enable forms of pedagogical structure that are difficult to sustain through self-guided practice alone.

In both the TikTok dance system and the adaptive coaching prototype, the most effective components were those that decomposed movement into manageable units and guided learners through incremental practice sequences. Automation made it possible to generate and deliver these structures at scale, but their value derived from pedagogical design rather than from automation itself.

#### 9.1.4 Evaluation becomes the bottleneck once practice is legible.

The adaptive coaching chapter makes clear that the move from structured lesson delivery to coaching requires more than generating encouraging language or reordering activities. It requires a believable account of the learner's performance, progress, and current needs. This bottleneck becomes visible only after interface legibility and practice structure have improved. Once learners know what to do next, the remaining weakness is whether the system can produce feedback that a learner can actually act on, a challenge closely tied to the role of feedback in motor learning systems ([Sigrist et al. 2013a](11-references.md#ref-sigrist2013augmented)). As discussed in [section 7.7](07-toward-an-adaptive-virtual-dance-coach.md#sec:motion-quantification), this challenge is compounded by sensing limitations, incomplete visibility, and the need for metrics that remain robust under imperfect observational data.

This pattern also appears in prior work comparing automated feedback with human instruction. Kawełczyk et al. ([2025](11-references.md#ref-kawelczyk2025morethanprecision)) find that a motion-capture-based system can detect alignment errors more precisely than a human instructor, yet learners report greater perceived improvement and preference with the instructor. This result reinforces the present finding: once practice structure is sufficiently clear, the limiting factor is not access to error signals, but whether those signals function as meaningful coaching.

#### 9.1.5 Adaptive coaching components proved tightly coupled in practice.

The later stages of the project revealed that adaptive coaching depends on the interaction of several components: motion representation, performance evaluation, pedagogical policy, and learner modeling. Improvements in one area did not translate cleanly into system-level gains without corresponding progress in the others. For example, more structured lessons made deficiencies in evaluation more apparent, and more expressive feedback required more reliable underlying metrics.

An open question is what a loosely coupled adaptive system would look like in this domain. It is plausible that some forms of adaptation could rely more heavily on learner self-report, heuristic progression rules, or partial evaluation signals rather than requiring a fully unified model of movement understanding. Exploring such designs may reveal alternative paths toward adaptive coaching that avoid some of the tight dependencies encountered here.

A further theme across both prior work and the systems developed in this thesis is the role of difficulty in shaping learning. The case studies restructure how movement is presented and practiced, without modifying the motion itself, yet they still regulate the learner's exposure to complexity. Representation, sequencing, and feedback therefore operate in close coordination with the alignment between task demands and learner capability, a relationship formalized in motor-learning accounts such as the challenge point framework ([Guadagnoli and Lee 2004](11-references.md#ref-guadagnoli2004challenge)).

### 9.2 Interdisciplinary Friction as Methodological Signal

The sequence of reframings across this dissertation was not merely a matter of gradually improving paper positioning. It reflects a deeper methodological condition of the problem itself. Expressive movement learning sits at the boundary of several partially incompatible knowledge regimes: computational research asks what can be represented, evaluated, and generalized; HCI asks what kinds of systems and interactions create meaningful support; learning sciences ask how instructional structure and theory explain learning effects; and embodied or artistic traditions ask what kinds of movement knowledge remain situated, experiential, and resistant to reduction. As a result, the object of study keeps shifting shape as the work moves closer to the lived phenomenon.

The exploratory spatial-interface chapter reinforces this point from a different angle. The Quest-based ASL follow-on surfaces the accessibility costs of richer sensing; the haptic probe shows that lower-visual-load feedback channels are not automatically interpretable; and the NAO retargeting probe exposes how expressive intent changes when motion is translated across bodies with different constraints. None of these probes stands as a core contribution on its own. Together, however, they show that modality, embodiment, and accessibility reshape what counts as legible or coach-like support.

Seen in this light, several of the thesis artifacts function as what Star and Griesemer call boundary objects: they are flexible enough to be meaningful in different social worlds while remaining stable enough to coordinate work across them ([Star and Griesemer 1989](11-references.md#ref-star1989institutional_ecology)). Intermediate motion representations, practice plans, and motion metrics each play that role here. They allow movement to be discussed simultaneously as something to compute, something to teach, and something to experience, even though those framings do not fully agree on what matters most. Gorman's account of trading zones offers a related lens ([Gorman 2002](11-references.md#ref-gorman2002tradingzones)). The dissertation can be understood as an effort to build a locally workable vocabulary among HCI, movement pedagogy, learning science, and embodied practice without assuming that these domains collapse into a single unified theory.

That translational work also helps explain the particular research stance the project required. Collins and Evans argue that interdisciplinary inquiry often depends on interactional expertise: enough fluency in another community's language and evaluative norms to participate meaningfully without claiming full contributory expertise in every domain ([Collins and Evans 2017](11-references.md#ref-collins2017bearing)). That description fits the dissertation's trajectory closely. The work repeatedly had to translate between technical representations, pedagogical rationales, and movement-specific experiential concerns.

The review history surrounding these projects can therefore be read as methodological signal rather than as external biography. Across venues, three tensions recurred: requests for stronger theoretical grounding when the work was read through learning-science expectations, skepticism about novelty when system contributions looked more like integration than a new technical primitive, and calls for richer qualitative or practice-grounded analysis when embodied learning claims were evaluated through narrower empirical conventions. The eventual acceptance of parts of this research in other venues does not negate those tensions; it shows that the work became legible differently under different evaluative cultures. Recent embodied-learning methodology work describes a similar problem: research in complex practice settings must balance rigor with tractable implications while contending with difficulties of framing, evaluation, and multimodal evidence capture ([Price et al. 2024](11-references.md#ref-price2024methodological_challenge_muesums)).

### 9.3 Limits of Correctness in Expressive Movement

A persistent question across the thesis is how movement should be evaluated, and what counts as "good" performance. The systems in this work primarily rely on geometric comparison to reference motion, and any notion of functional success is derived from that geometric alignment.

This focus is useful because replication is often an important stage in learning. It provides a tractable basis for structured practice, feedback, and automatic lesson generation. However, expressive movement is not exhausted by replication. In artistic and social movement domains, variation can be meaningful rather than erroneous, and the values by which movement is judged can shift across communities, purposes, and aesthetic traditions.

This tension is visible in the broader literature as well. Movement-analysis traditions such as Laban Movement Analysis were developed precisely because "good" movement cannot be reduced to geometry alone ([Guest 2013](11-references.md#ref-guest2013labanotation); [Bernardet et al. 2019](11-references.md#ref-bernardet2019assessing)). Animation traditions treat timing, anticipation, follow-through, and exaggeration as tools for making motion read a certain way ([Lasseter 1987](11-references.md#ref-lasseter1987principles); [Chi et al. 2000](11-references.md#ref-chi2000emote)). HRI work on legibility similarly emphasizes that movement is interpreted by an observer situated in context ([Dragan et al. 2013](11-references.md#ref-dragan2013legiblity)).

This limitation becomes especially visible in systems that simplify choreography for novices. Such work shows that correctness is not always a meaningful objective when the target behavior exceeds the learner's current capability. In these cases, reducing coordination demands or altering temporal structure can produce more effective learning than enforcing fidelity to the original motion, reflecting both simplification-based approaches to choreography and motor-learning accounts of difficulty--skill alignment ([Han et al. 2026](11-references.md#ref-han2026makesimplemakedance); [Guadagnoli and Lee 2004](11-references.md#ref-guadagnoli2004challenge)).

The systems in this thesis therefore address a tractable subset of this broader design space. They support replication-oriented learning, decomposition of target motions, and structured refinement relative to examples or instructor-derived criteria. A future generation of systems would need richer movement-quality models, broader cultural grounding, and interaction designs that support exploration in addition to convergence.

### 9.4 Difficulty as a First-Class Design Variable

Across the literature, distinct strategies have emerged for managing the difficulty of expressive movement. Some approaches focus on sequencing, decomposing a task into smaller units and organizing them into progressively more complex structures ([Yang et al. 2013](11-references.md#ref-yang2013twophasedancelesson)). Others operate through transformation, directly modifying the movement to reduce coordination demands while preserving perceptual anchors for the learner ([Han et al. 2026](11-references.md#ref-han2026makesimplemakedance)).

The systems presented in this thesis operate through a third mechanism: restructuring the learner's interaction with a fixed target motion. By segmenting, reordering, and selectively emphasizing aspects of the movement, they shape how difficult the task feels in practice without altering the underlying choreography.

This view of difficulty also aligns with several adjacent theoretical traditions, though they arise from different literatures and should not be collapsed into a single account. In motor learning, the challenge point framework argues that practice conditions should be matched to the interaction of task difficulty and learner skill in order to maximize learning-relevant information ([Guadagnoli and Lee 2004](11-references.md#ref-guadagnoli2004challenge)). In contrast, educational theories such as the zone of proximal development and scaffolding describe how support is structured around tasks that learners can perform with assistance, with that support gradually withdrawn over time ([Vygotsky 1978](11-references.md#ref-vygotsky1978mind); [Wood et al. 1976](11-references.md#ref-wood1976tutoring); [Pol et al. 2010](11-references.md#ref-vandepol2010scaffolding)). For movement-learning systems, these traditions converge on a practical design principle: challenge should be calibrated, and support should be staged, so that learners are neither left with tasks beyond their current control nor held in overly simplified regimes that stall progression.

These approaches define a design space in which difficulty can be managed through sequencing, transformation, or interaction design. Most systems, including those developed here, emphasize one of these axes at a time. This separation limits their ability to respond to the full range of learner needs, and points toward approaches that combine adaptive feedback, interpretable representation, and control over task difficulty.

### 9.5 What This Thesis Establishes

This thesis establishes a concrete path toward computational support for expressive movement learning.

It shows, first, that interpretable representations are essential if automated systems are to provide meaningful movement feedback, consistent with prior work in movement notation and qualitative analysis ([Guest 2013](11-references.md#ref-guest2013labanotation); [Camurri, Volpe, et al. 2016](11-references.md#ref-camurri2016dancer)). Second, it shows that these representations must be coupled with pedagogically structured practice routines if they are to support learning at scale, extending approaches that emphasize staged progression and structured practice design ([Weng et al. 2025](11-references.md#ref-weng2025_sop_basketball); [Wang et al. 2025](11-references.md#ref-wang2025_mrdrum)). Third, it shows that the move from structured lesson delivery toward adaptive coaching depends on learner modeling and motion evaluation that are interpretable enough to ground system decisions, echoing prior work on feedback and instructional support in motor learning ([Sigrist et al. 2013a](11-references.md#ref-sigrist2013augmented)).

The resulting contribution is cumulative rather than monolithic. The thesis identifies a practical trajectory: begin with interpretable domain-specific feedback, generalize representation, operationalize pedagogy in scalable lesson generation, and then extend toward adaptive coaching. The final step proved substantially more difficult than the earlier ones, because it depends on tightly coupled advances in representation, evaluation, pedagogy, and learner modeling.
