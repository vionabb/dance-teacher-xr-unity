## 1 Abstract

Existing interactive systems for movement learning often face a trade-off between generality and instructional usefulness. Systems that provide rich, interpretable feedback are hand-authored for narrow domains, while more general systems rely on raw motion playback or minimal guidance with limited support for learning. This tension is especially pronounced in expressive movement domains such as dance and sign language, where motion is spatial, temporal, and difficult to summarize in words.

This thesis argues that movement becomes more learnable when computational systems leverage a combination of representation, pedagogical structure, and feedback. It develops this perspective through a sequence of systems that probe different aspects of the problem. The first explores how symbolic representations enable interpretable, feature-level feedback in a mixed-reality system for teaching American Sign Language. The second examines how raw motion capture can be transformed into structure through temporal segmentation and intermediate representations. The third investigates how instructional structure can be generated from online dance videos, evaluating decomposition and sequencing. The fourth examines feedback, exploring an adaptive coaching system that reveals the gap between structured lesson delivery and coach-like guidance.

Taken together, these studies show that the central challenge in computational support for expressive movement learning is not only sensing or displaying motion, but making movement legible as something that can be practiced, interpreted, and refined. Across systems, this repeatedly involves decomposing motion into intermediate representations, organizing practice into structured progressions, and connecting these representations to feedback. These patterns point to underlying constraints of the problem space and suggest a cumulative path toward movement learning systems grounded in interpretable representation, structured pedagogy, and adaptive feedback.
