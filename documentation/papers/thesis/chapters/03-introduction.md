## 3 Introduction

<a id="chap:introduction"></a>

Learning new movement skills plays an important role in human experience, supporting cognitive, mental, social, and physical well-being ([Mikkelsen et al. 2017](11-references.md#ref-mikkelsen2017exercise); [Tomporowski and Pesce 2019](11-references.md#ref-tomporowski2019exerciseCognitionBenefit); [Shi and Feng 2022](11-references.md#ref-shi2022motor_cogbenefits); [Xu et al. 2024](11-references.md#ref-xu2024emotionInGait); [Çetin and Çevikbaş 2020](11-references.md#ref-zeynep2020creativedance_emotionalexpression); [Grau 2016](11-references.md#ref-grau2016danceidentity_postcolonialworld); [Jefferies et al. 2019](11-references.md#ref-jefferies2019physicalliteracy_resilience)). Human-computer interaction has produced many systems for physical skill training, but most are either narrowly tailored to a particular task or comparatively light-touch in the support they provide ([Mocco et al. 2024](11-references.md#ref-mocco2024enhancingphysicalactivity_vr); [Cheatham et al. 2017](11-references.md#ref-cheatham2017wearable_weightloss); [Marco-Ahulló et al. 2024](11-references.md#ref-ahullo2024feedbackfreq_posturalcontrol); [Fitton et al. 2023](11-references.md#ref-fitton2023dancelearning_avatarcustomization); [Iwamoto et al. 2022](11-references.md#ref-iwamoto2022fieldhockey_pushpassfeedback); [Kelly et al. 2010](11-references.md#ref-kelly2010virtualgolf)). This tradeoff becomes especially visible in expressive movement domains such as dance and sign language, where motion is spatial, temporal, and difficult to summarize in words.

This thesis investigates how interactive systems can better support the learning of expressive movement. I focus on full-body movement skills in which the body itself is the primary medium of action, and set aside object-mediated skills for future work. Across the systems developed in this dissertation, a consistent pattern emerges: movement becomes more learnable when systems provide interpretable representations, structure practice in meaningful ways, and connect those representations to actionable feedback, rather than relying on raw motion playback or domain-specific hand-authoring alone.

The core challenge, then, is not only sensing or displaying movement. Raw motion signals, avatar animations, and videos can all communicate that a movement occurred, but they do not by themselves provide the abstractions, instructional structure, and feedback that help learners understand what to practice and how to improve. A cumulative path becomes visible: beginning with interpretable domain-specific feedback, expanding toward more general representations, organizing practice through structured lesson generation, and moving toward adaptive instruction informed by learner models and motion evaluation.

Across these systems, a fourth consideration emerges alongside representation, structure, and feedback: the alignment between task difficulty and learner capability. This alignment shapes how movement is interpreted, practiced, and refined, opening a broader design space for movement learning systems.

#### 3.1 Scope of "good" movement

For the purposes of this thesis, I focus primarily on systems that help learners reproduce and refine target movements in legible ways. This does not exhaust the question of what makes expressive movement "good" in broader artistic, cultural, or personal terms. Rather, I treat accurate replication, structured practice, and interpretable feedback as useful entry points into a larger design space that I return to in the discussion chapter. Motion replication is not the end state of expressive movement learning, but it is often an important first step toward adaptation, stylistic development, and mastery.

### 3.1 Expressive Movement as a Computational Problem

Expressive movement is difficult to define exhaustively because it is not reducible to a single target variable. Across artistic, social, and instructional settings, movement can communicate affect, intention, style, and affiliation, often simultaneously. Prior work in animation, robotics, dance computing, and movement analysis therefore suggests that expressive movement is not well captured by raw trajectories or coarse action labels alone. Researchers and practitioners instead return to intermediate descriptors: symbolic notation systems such as Labanotation and Laban Movement Analysis ([Guest 2013](11-references.md#ref-guest2013labanotation); [Bernardet et al. 2019](11-references.md#ref-bernardet2019assessing); [Wilke et al. 2005](11-references.md#ref-wilke2005labandancer); [Abe et al. 2017](11-references.md#ref-abe2017notationsystems_robotmovements)), computable movement qualities such as contraction, impulsiveness, and fluency ([Camurri, Volpe, et al. 2016](11-references.md#ref-camurri2016dancer); [Camurri, Canepa, et al. 2016](11-references.md#ref-camurri2016system)), and observer-facing concepts such as legibility ([Dragan et al. 2013](11-references.md#ref-dragan2013legiblity)). Animation research reinforces this perspective from the generative side: principles such as timing, anticipation, follow-through, and exaggeration are valued because they shape how motion is read by an audience ([Lasseter 1987](11-references.md#ref-lasseter1987principles); [Chi et al. 2000](11-references.md#ref-chi2000emote)).

This thesis takes up a deliberately narrower and more practical subset of that larger problem. Rather than proposing a complete theory of expressive movement, I ask which abstractions are interpretable enough for learners, tractable enough for computation, and concrete enough to ground instruction. The recurring representational candidates that matter throughout the dissertation are movement qualities, temporal shaping, spatial extent, decompositions into phases or primitives, and observer-facing legibility. The contribution of this work is to show how interactive systems can operationalize a useful portion of this space for movement teaching.

The cumulative structure of this dissertation is also methodological. Each case study approaches the same general problem from a different disciplinary angle because no single framework fully captures expressive movement learning. Computational system-building clarifies what can be represented, sensed, and automated; learning-science and pedagogical perspectives clarify how practice should be structured; and embodied and artistic perspectives clarify why movement quality and experience cannot be reduced to correctness alone. The trajectory of the work reflects an effort to make expressive movement legible across these partially overlapping perspectives.

### 3.2 Research Questions

This dissertation is organized around four chapter-facing research questions. Each addresses a specific design or evaluation problem while advancing the broader argument that effective computational support for expressive movement depends on the joint development of interpretable representation, pedagogical structure, and feedback:

1. In a domain with strong domain-specific notation such as sign language, how can a movement-teaching system translate raw sensing into interpretable, actionable feedback?

2. Beyond domain-specific notation, what intermediate representations make complex 3D motion more intelligible to learners across different modalities?

3. When lessons are generated automatically from online dance videos, which aspects of pedagogical structure improve learning, and how do different forms of instructional support contribute to that process?

4. Once lesson structure becomes coherent and usable, what additional machinery is required for feedback that is specific, trustworthy, and adaptive enough to resemble coaching?

### 3.3 Dissertation Structure

I develop these questions through four main case studies. [Reference](04-teaching-american-sign-language-in-mixed-reality.md#chap:ubicomp2019) examines how an interactive system can turn raw sensing into legible movement feedback in a domain with an existing symbolic vocabulary, using the Hamburg Notation System for American Sign Language. [Reference](05-decomposition-and-structured-representation-of-human-motion-capture.md#chap:iser2020) then explores more general abstractions, including temporal segmentation, skeletal isolation, and intermediate representations derived from motion capture data. [Reference](06-enhancing-the-educational-potential-of-tiktok-dance-videos.md#chap:chi-tiktok-dance) shifts to pedagogy, evaluating which forms of automatically generated lesson structure help learners acquire dances from online videos at scale. Finally, [Reference](07-toward-an-adaptive-virtual-dance-coach.md#chap:chi27-dancecoach) examines feedback, asking what remains unresolved once a learning journey becomes more coherent and how systems might move from structured lesson delivery toward adaptive coaching.

The thesis then broadens beyond these case studies. [Reference](08-spatial-interface-investigations.md#chap:miscellaneous) presents exploratory spatial-interface and embodiment probes that investigate interface techniques for expressive movement teaching and reveal how tightly coupled representation, pedagogy, feedback, and embodiment can be in practice. [Reference](09-discussion.md#chap:discussion) synthesizes cross-cutting lessons and revisits the tension between measurable correctness and open-ended expression. [Reference](10-future-directions.md#chap:future-directions) outlines directions for future work building on this trajectory toward more capable movement learning systems.

### 3.4 Related Work

<a id="chap:relatedwork"></a>

#### 3.4.1 Overview

A large body of work has examined motor learning, and a smaller but still substantial literature has explored interactive systems for movement skill learning. Rather than attempting a comprehensive review, this section maps the literature streams that are most relevant to the dissertation's problem framing: how expressive movement can be described, computed, and shaped in ways that support learning.

Across these areas, three recurring strategies emerge. First, researchers construct symbolic or qualitative vocabularies that describe movement structure at a level between raw kinematics and domain-specific action labels. Second, computational approaches attempt to infer expressive qualities directly from motion signals. Third, animation and human-robot interaction work focuses on shaping motion for an observer, emphasizing how movement is read and interpreted. Together, these strategies define the representational and perceptual foundations on which computational movement-teaching systems are built, and motivate the shift toward structured pedagogy and feedback explored later in this section.

#### 3.4.2 Movement Notation and Representational Systems

One longstanding strategy for understanding expressive movement is to build a vocabulary for describing how movement is performed. Labanotation and the broader Laban Movement Analysis tradition are especially important because they aim to describe movement at a level between raw kinematics and domain-specific action names ([Guest 2013](11-references.md#ref-guest2013labanotation); [Bernardet et al. 2019](11-references.md#ref-bernardet2019assessing)). In computing-oriented work, this has made them attractive as a bridge between human legibility and computational structure. Laban-inspired representations have been used to translate dance notation into animation, to generate robot motion from symbolic scores, and to segment motion capture data using qualitative features rather than purely geometric ones ([Wilke et al. 2005](11-references.md#ref-wilke2005labandancer); [Abe et al. 2017](11-references.md#ref-abe2017notationsystems_robotmovements); [Bouchard and Badler 2015](11-references.md#ref-bouchard2015segmenting)).

This line of work highlights a central constraint: expressive movement resists description in terms of positions alone. Researchers repeatedly return to structured, qualitative representations because they make movement legible in ways raw kinematics do not, even though they are labor-intensive to learn and difficult to compute robustly from sensor data.

#### 3.4.3 Computational Approaches to Movement Quality

Another line of work attempts to infer expressive qualities directly from motion signals. Camurri and collaborators, for example, propose multi-layer computational frameworks in which low-level kinematic measures support higher-level interpretations of movement quality ([Camurri, Volpe, et al. 2016](11-references.md#ref-camurri2016dancer)). Related systems compute features such as contraction, impulsiveness, and motion fluency to make movement quality visible to performers and teachers ([Camurri, Canepa, et al. 2016](11-references.md#ref-camurri2016system); [Rizzo et al. 2018](11-references.md#ref-rizzo2018wholodance_danceeducation)). Perception research points in a similar direction: observers can reliably read affective or stylistic information from sparse motion displays, suggesting that expressive qualities are partially recoverable from general movement dynamics rather than domain-specific symbolic knowledge alone ([Pollick et al. 2001](11-references.md#ref-pollick2001affect)).

This work demonstrates that expressive aspects of movement can be approximated computationally without collapsing to action recognition. At the same time, it exposes a persistent gap: many computed descriptors remain context-sensitive, difficult to validate across domains, and only weakly aligned with the kinds of feedback a learner can act on.

#### 3.4.4 Communicative and Generative Models of Expressivity

Animation and human-robot interaction provide a complementary perspective by focusing on how motion is shaped for an observer. Traditional animation principles such as timing, anticipation, follow-through, and exaggeration function as a practical theory of communicative motion, shaping how action, weight, and intention are perceived ([Lasseter 1987](11-references.md#ref-lasseter1987principles)). Computational models such as EMOTE extend this idea by treating expressive qualities as controllable parameters that modify how a movement is executed rather than what action is performed ([Chi et al. 2000](11-references.md#ref-chi2000emote)). Work on embodied agents similarly emphasizes that expression depends on how behavior unfolds over time, not only on the behavior itself ([Pelachaud 2009](11-references.md#ref-pelachaud2009modelling)).

Human-robot interaction sharpens this observer-centered view. Dragan et al.'s distinction between predictable and legible motion shows that movement can be optimized for human inference, not only for task efficiency ([Dragan et al. 2013](11-references.md#ref-dragan2013legiblity)). Taken together, this work suggests that supporting expressive movement is not only a matter of capturing motion faithfully, but of choosing representations and transformations that make movement structure readable enough to guide interpretation and action.

#### 3.4.5 Pedagogical Structure and Coaching Systems

More recent HCI work on physical skill learning connects these representational questions to instructional design. Rather than treating movement learning as repetition plus generic feedback, several systems make pedagogical structure itself a primary design object. Weng et al., for example, ground AI basketball feedback in an expert-derived standard operating procedure so that system output remains legible in coaching terms ([Weng et al. 2025](11-references.md#ref-weng2025_sop_basketball)). Wang et al. derive a staged "micro-progression" framework for drumming, decomposing rhythm and coordination into structured learning steps ([Wang et al. 2025](11-references.md#ref-wang2025_mrdrum)). These systems show that scalable coaching depends not only on sensing or interface design, but on how domain knowledge is operationalized into interpretable progressions and activity sequences.

Beyond representation, this literature introduces three closely related concerns: how learners think about movement (cognition), how performance is interpreted (evaluation), and how guidance is structured over time (instructional adaptation).

One line of work examines how sensing-based feedback systems compare directly to human instruction. Kawełczyk et al. ([2025](11-references.md#ref-kawelczyk2025morethanprecision)) evaluate a motion-capture-based "digital mirror" for beginner dance learning and find that while the system provides more precise detection of alignment errors, participants report greater perceived improvement and preference when guided by a human instructor. This result highlights a recurring limitation of correctness-oriented feedback: increased diagnostic precision does not necessarily translate into feedback that functions as effective coaching.

A complementary but often underrepresented thread within this literature emphasizes that movement learning is not only a matter of repeated execution, but of supporting cognition about movement. Studies of dance practice and motor learning describe techniques such as marking and cognitive rehearsal, in which learners partially perform or mentally simulate motion sequences to reduce working-memory load and stabilize a conceptual model of the movement before full execution ([Rivière et al. 2018](11-references.md#ref-riviere2018dancers); [Sattelmayer et al. 2016](11-references.md#ref-sattelmayer2016); [Warburton et al. 2013](11-references.md#ref-warburton2013_markingbenefits)). While these strategies are often implicit in expert practice, they are rarely made explicit in computational systems. This gap becomes especially relevant in the design of scalable coaching systems, where learners may lack both domain knowledge and effective practice strategies.

This work also sharpens an evaluation challenge that recurs throughout the dissertation. If a system claims to support learning rather than immediate performance, then retention, transfer, and learning trajectory become necessary criteria. Villa et al.'s study of electrical muscle stimulation illustrates this shift by evaluating consolidation and transfer rather than relying solely on short-term gains ([Villa et al. 2025](11-references.md#ref-villa2025_ems_motorlearning)). Taken together, these results reinforce that evaluation must be aligned with learning outcomes rather than short-term performance, and must produce signals that learners and systems can act on.

Prior work on augmented feedback further suggests that the timing and frequency of guidance are themselves central design variables. Concurrent and frequent feedback can accelerate early-stage performance, but may hinder longer-term learning by fostering dependency, motivating strategies such as fading feedback and self-controlled feedback schedules ([Sigrist et al. 2013a](11-references.md#ref-sigrist2013augmented), [2013b](11-references.md#ref-sigrist2013_rowtask)). These considerations are closely tied to a broader distinction between movement performance and movement learning. Immediate performance can fluctuate with attention, fatigue, or context, whereas learning refers to more durable changes that persist over time and transfer across conditions ([Pyke 2012](11-references.md#ref-pyke2012coachingexcellence_movementphases)). As a result, systems that aim to support learning must account not only for how well a movement is performed in the moment, but for how feedback and practice structure shape longer-term skill acquisition.

A similar instructional pattern appears in participatory design work on accessible dance instruction, but from a very different methodological starting point. Co-design workshops with blind and low-vision learners and instructors produce systems that decompose routines into named movement units, organize instruction into staged phases, distribute guidance across modalities, and separate support for what to do from support for how to do it ([Das et al. 2026](11-references.md#ref-das2026accessibleasynchronousdance)). Interpretable representations, structured pedagogy, and modality-specific guidance therefore appear not as artifacts of individual systems, but as recurring responses to the problem of making complex movement learnable.

A useful theoretical lens for this instructional layer comes from scaffolding research and the zone of proximal development, where learning is supported by structuring assistance around tasks that the learner cannot yet perform independently but can accomplish with guidance ([Vygotsky 1978](11-references.md#ref-vygotsky1978mind); [Wood et al. 1976](11-references.md#ref-wood1976tutoring)). Later work on scaffolding sharpens this into a more operational account, emphasizing contingent support, fading, and transfer of responsibility over time ([Pol et al. 2010](11-references.md#ref-vandepol2010scaffolding)). Although these ideas emerged primarily from general learning-science and sociocultural traditions rather than motor-learning research, they map productively onto movement-teaching systems that decompose tasks, stage support, and gradually withdraw guidance as learners gain competence.

Taken together, this literature defines the dissertation's problem space: how expressive movement can be represented, made legible, evaluated, and organized into teachable structure. The chapters that follow narrow from this framing to more local precedents. [Reference](06-enhancing-the-educational-potential-of-tiktok-dance-videos.md#chap:chi-tiktok-dance) focuses on video-based dance learning and motor-learning-grounded lesson generation, while [Reference](07-toward-an-adaptive-virtual-dance-coach.md#chap:chi27-dancecoach) turns to learner modeling, feedback, and coaching-system design.

#### 3.4.6 Difficulty Modulation and Capability Alignment

A complementary line of work extends these concerns beyond representation and sequencing to the alignment between movement difficulty and learner capability. These systems treat the target movement as something that can be adjusted, either through the structure of practice or through transformations of the motion itself, in order to maintain an appropriate level of challenge.

Early work in this space explored how dance instruction can be structured to reduce cognitive load. Yang et al. ([2013](11-references.md#ref-yang2013twophasedancelesson)) propose a two-phase lesson generation approach in which a dance is first decomposed into reusable patterns, then reassembled through an adaptive sequencing process that responds to learner performance. This formulation draws on cognitive load theory, emphasizing prerequisite structure and incremental progression.

Recent work extends this idea to the motion itself. Han et al. ([2026](11-references.md#ref-han2026makesimplemakedance)) identify recurring sources of difficulty in dance, including multi-limb coordination, asymmetry, and rapid directional changes, and introduce methods for generating simplified versions of choreography. These transformations preserve perceptual anchors such as rhythm and salient poses while reducing execution demands.

Across this line of work, difficulty appears as a relational property: it emerges from the interaction between a movement and a learner. This perspective expands the design space of movement learning systems beyond representation, sequencing, and evaluation, suggesting that systems may also reshape the complexity of the task itself.

A closely related perspective from motor learning is the challenge point framework, which argues that learning depends on the interaction between nominal task difficulty and the learner's current skill, such that practice is most productive when challenge is neither trivial nor overwhelming ([Guadagnoli and Lee 2004](11-references.md#ref-guadagnoli2004challenge)). This account is not identical to scaffolding or the zone of proximal development, which operate at a broader instructional level, but the three perspectives converge on a common implication: movement-learning systems should not treat task difficulty as fixed. Instead, they should regulate challenge and support relative to the learner's current capability.
